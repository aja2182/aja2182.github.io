Past and Ongoing Work

1. Young adults in substance use treatment and recovery

Young adults ages 18–25 have high rates of substance use but are less likely than older adults to start or stay in treatment. With colleagues at NYU and the NYS Office of Addiction Services and Supports, I built a linked treatment registry and Medicaid claims cohort to study how treatment systems serve this group. Alongside it, I've interviewed young adults and the providers who treat them. This work shows that young adults are a developmentally distinct population, not just younger versions of older patients.

2. ADHD and substance use disorder

ADHD is common among young people in addiction treatment, yet it often goes unrecognized and untreated. Through the ATLAS-NY study (ADHD in Treatment: a Linked Administrative Study, New York), I examine who receives an ADHD diagnosis and medication, whether treatment helps people stay in care, and which organizational factors shape these patterns.

In progress: papers on ADHD documentation and medication among young adults entering substance use treatment, and on whether ADHD medication helps young people stay in care.

3. Policy, stigma, and access to care

I study how state policy and organizational practice shape treatment access for pregnant women, sexual minority adults, and people involved in the criminal justice system. Topics include reproductive rights and access to medication for opioid use disorder, and how stigma persists inside treatment programs.

Rehab Kids: Coming of Age in a Drug Crisis. This book follows young people's substance use careers as they unfold alongside mental health challenges and a shifting treatment and recovery landscape. Drawing on interviews with young adults in specialty treatment, in community-based programs, and outside both, it shows how some careers stay socially anchored while others narrow into isolation. It also traces how young people encounter a treatment system that often opens only in crisis: some are turned back early by stigma and eligibility rules, while others are pushed in late by families and the state, often after an overdose or other emergency makes their use visible. Throughout, young people describe using substances to cope with distress and grief, manage mood and attention, find belonging, and perform competence at work, at school, and in relationships, which makes stopping feel like losing the tools they use to grow up.

Getting It Together: ADHD and Pharmaceutical Adulthood in an Age of Uncertainty. A study of adults ages 18–35 who come to see themselves through an ADHD diagnosis received in adulthood. The book examines how a disorder once tied to childhood, and the psychostimulants used to treat it, become a way of growing up at a time when the transition to adulthood has become increasingly protracted and precarious. This extended period of becoming creates the conditions of possibility for pharmaceutical experimentation, even as adulthood itself becomes an ever more elusive achievement.
